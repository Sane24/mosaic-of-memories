Thirteen photographs from one afternoon around Berkeley, a few dozen points I matched by hand, and a 3×3 matrix for every pair. This is my write-up for the image mosaicing project, in two reels. Reel A is Part A: recovering homographies, warping with my own nearest-neighbor and bilinear samplers, rectifying three views of flat objects, blending three panoramas, and, as an extra, wrapping a five-frame panorama onto a cylinder. Reel B is Part B: the same panoramas again with no clicking at all, using Harris corners, small patch descriptors and RANSAC.

01 / A.1 Shoot the pictures

Fragments

Three sets of three overlapping frames, laid out like a contact sheet. The dashed outline on each print is the part of the scene its neighbor also saw, computed from the recovered homography.

Everything was shot on my iPhone 14 Pro in a single afternoon. For each mosaic I took a middle frame and then turned left and right from the same spot, so each set is three frames taken within a few seconds of each other (the sunset set spans about a minute and a half). The campus and café sets use the main 24 mm-equivalent lens. For the sunset I used the 2× setting (46 to 48 mm equivalent), which makes the bay bigger in frame but leaves less overlap between shots.

A homography lines two photos up exactly only when the camera rotates about its own optical center or the scene is flat. A phone never pivots perfectly about its lens, so how well each set works depends mostly on how far away the subject is. The phone also picked its own exposure for every frame, which matters later: in the sunset set the shutter speed ranges from 1/10870 s to 1/4184 s.

02 / A.2 Recover homographies

Finding the same points

Two photos of the same place disagree about where everything is. A handful of landmarks they agree on is enough to work out the rest.

For each pair I looked for landmarks I could place exactly in both photos: corners of window frames, letters carved into the facade, the top of a Golden Gate tower, the corners of the menu screens. I tried to spread them across the whole overlap instead of clustering them on one object, since points bunched together only constrain the transformation near that spot. Each side frame is matched to the middle frame, so every mosaic has two point sets and two homographies.

I placed the points on the full-resolution 4032 × 3024 photos and saved them to JSON in original-pixel coordinates. The pipeline works on a downsized copy (long side 1800 px), so it rescales the points on load and never needs them re-picked. All the pixel errors below are at that 1800 px working size.

    Hover or focus a landmark to follow its thread. Numbers in brackets are the leftover error for that point, in pixels.

    Interactive on the live site. The printed version shows the saved correspondence figures below.

    the model

    A homography maps a point (x, y) in one photo to (u, v) in the other through homogeneous coordinates:

    wuwvw = h11h12h13h21h22h23h31h321 xy1

    Setting h33 = 1 fixes the scale, leaving eight unknowns. Dividing out w and multiplying through gives two equations that are linear in the unknowns:

    h11x + h12y + h13 − h31xu − h32yu = u h21x + h22y + h23 − h31xv − h32yv = v

    stacking every correspondence

    Ah = b

    x1y11000−x1u1−y1u1 000x1y11−x1v1−y1v1 ⋮⋮⋮⋮⋮⋮⋮⋮ xnyn1000−xnun−ynun 000xnyn1−xnvn−ynvn h11h12h13h21h22h23h31h32 = u1v1⋮unvn

    Every pair of points adds two rows, so n correspondences give a 2n × 8 system. Four points are the minimum, and with exactly four the system is square and the homography passes through them exactly. With 7 to 14 points per pair the system is overdetermined, and computeH solves it by least squares (np.linalg.lstsq, minimizing ‖Ah − b‖²). That is the point of using extra matches: a pixel or two of error in where I put a point gets averaged against the others instead of going straight into H.

    One practical detail: before building A, I shift and scale each point set so its centroid is at the origin and its average distance from it is √2, then undo that transform on the result. Raw pixel coordinates make the xu columns about a million times larger than the constant column, and the normalization keeps the solve well conditioned.

    03 / A.3 Warp the images

    Shifting perspective

    A photo rarely sees a flat thing straight on. If I know four corners of something that is really a rectangle, one homography turns it back to face me.

    I rectified three things: a book cover and two printed menus standing on a counter. For each I picked the four corners (top-left, top-right, bottom-right, bottom-left of the real object) and mapped them to the corners of an upright rectangle. With exactly four points, computeH gets a square 8 × 8 system and the corners land exactly where I asked.

    I did not want to guess the rectangle's proportions, so I estimated them from the photo. The EXIF data says 24 mm equivalent, about 1248 px at the working size. Shooting a ray from the camera through each corner and requiring the four 3D points to form a parallelogram fixes the true side lengths up to scale. That gives width/height 0.659 for the book and 0.775 for the front menu. The menu looks like a US-letter sheet, which would be 8.5/11 = 0.773, and the recovered corner angles are 89.3° and 90.1°, so I trust the estimates.

    the hidden corner

    This menu's left edge is behind the front stand, so two of its corners are not visible. I fit straight lines to the top, right and bottom edges of the sheet. The right edge and a printed vertical line on the sheet meet far below the photo at the vertical vanishing point, and the hidden left edge has to pass through that point too. Assuming the sheet is the same size as the front menu (same stand, same paper), that fixes where the left edge is. The bottom-left corner it predicts lands within about 25 px of where the sheet visibly meets the stand, which is a good check. The strip on the left of the result is the front stand, which really is in front of the sheet.

    Why inverse warping

    Both warps work backwards. For every pixel of the output I apply H−1 to find where it came from in the source photo and sample there. Pushing source pixels forward through H instead would leave holes wherever the warp stretches the image, and pile several pixels onto one where it shrinks. Going backwards, every output pixel gets exactly one value. Output pixels whose source location falls outside the photo are marked invalid in a mask rather than painted black.

    The source location is almost never a whole pixel. warpImageNearestNeighbor rounds it to the closest pixel center. warpImageBilinear takes the four pixels around it and weights them by (1−dx)(1−dy), dx(1−dy), (1−dx)dy and dx dy, where dx and dy are the fractional offsets. Both are written with NumPy indexing and arithmetic only.

    Quality and speed

    At normal viewing size the two results look almost identical, because these rectifications only enlarge the photo slightly. The difference shows up on edges once you zoom in: nearest neighbor gives jagged, blocky diagonals and bilinear gives smooth ones.

    Bilinear is slower because it reads four pixels and does several multiplies per output pixel instead of one lookup. Median of three runs on my machine: 31 ms vs 69 ms for the book and 51 ms vs 108 ms for the menu, so bilinear costs about 2.2× to 2.1× more. Both are fast enough not to matter here, so I used bilinear for everything else, including the mosaics.

    04 / A.4 Blend into mosaics

    Stitching the world back together

    Each mosaic lives in its middle frame's coordinates. The side frames are warped into that frame by chaining their homographies, and everything is blended on one shared canvas.

    To size the canvas, I push the four corners of every frame through its homography, take the bounding box, and offset the output so its top-left pixel sits at that box's minimum (usually negative) corner. Each frame is then inverse-warped onto the full canvas with bilinear sampling and its own validity mask. Before blending I scale each side frame so its median color in the overlap matches the middle frame, then feather all three together (details in section 05). The bow-tie shape is real: a flat projection stretches anything far from the middle frame's viewing direction, so the outer corners fan out.

    On the live site each close-up below is a draggable before/after slider, and clicking any panorama reveals the original photo behind that spot. The printed version shows the two close-ups side by side.

    05 / A.4 Blending, plus bells & whistles

    The invisible seams

    A mask decides what each frame is allowed to show. Blending decides how much.

    Once all three frames are on the same canvas, something has to decide what happens where they overlap. Pasting one on top of the other (the hard-seam versions above) leaves a visible edge wherever brightness or alignment differs even slightly.

    Instead, each frame gets a weight that is 1 at its center and falls off linearly to 0 at its border. I compute it as the distance to the nearest edge of the original photo, measured in that photo's own pixels, so it warps along with the frame. The output pixel is then the weighted average of every frame that covers it:

    I(p) = Σi mi(p) wi(p) Ii(p)Σi mi(p) wi(p)

    mi is the validity mask from the inverse warp: 1 if the pixel really came from inside photo i, 0 if it fell off the edge. That is what keeps empty canvas from being averaged in, and it is why a genuinely dark pixel (the black menu screens, the shadowed rooftops) still counts as data.

    06 / A.5 Bells & whistles

    Beyond the plane

    Five frames of the same sunset cover about 133° of horizon. A flat image plane cannot hold that gracefully, so I wrapped the mosaic onto a cylinder instead.

    Plate I only used three of the five sunset frames I took. For this I added the other two, matched each new pair by hand like before, and chained everything into the middle frame, mosaic_1a-1b.jpg: 9 points for mosaic_1z.jpg → mosaic_1a.jpg (mean error 1.28 px), 10 for mosaic_1a.jpg → mosaic_1a-1b.jpg (0.47 px), 10 for mosaic_1b.jpg → mosaic_1a-1b.jpg (1.22 px), and plate I's 8 points for mosaic_1c.jpg → mosaic_1b.jpg. The two outer frames on the right were taken about a minute and a half after the others, so the sun moved a little and the phone was tilted slightly differently. That shows up as the step in the top edge.

    The cylinder mapping only needs the homographies I already had. An output pixel (u, v) on a cylinder of radius f around the middle camera is the viewing ray (sin θ, v/f, cos θ) with θ = u/f. That ray hits the middle frame's image plane at x = cx + f tan θ, y = cy + v/cos θ, and Hi−1 takes that point back into photo i. So every output pixel is sampled once, straight from the original photo, with the same bilinear sampler as everything else. I never build the flat mosaic and re-warp it, which would blur twice and throw away the detail at the stretched edges.

    Distortion

    On the flat plane, horizontal distance grows like tan θ, so the frames furthest from the middle are pulled outward. Near the left end, each pixel of the original photo is blown up to as much as 4.67× its size, and the canvas fans into a bow-tie. On the cylinder, distance grows with the angle itself, so every frame keeps roughly its own size and the horizon stays straight. The price is that straight lines that are not vertical or on the horizon now curve gently, like the rooftop edges near the bottom.

    The radius is a real choice. Below the true focal length the horizon bows into an arc, because the mapping no longer matches how the camera saw the scene, and everything gets squeezed (stretch under 1 means photo pixels are being thrown away). Above it the result drifts back toward the planar look. At exactly the EXIF focal length, 2392 px, the horizon is flat, verticals stay vertical, and the stretch is 1.06× in the median and 1.21× at worst, so almost every output pixel is one photo pixel. That is the one I picked.

    Coverage and quality

    Only 56% of the planar canvas has any image in it; the rest is the empty wedges around the bow-tie. The chosen cylinder fills 86% of a much smaller canvas (5,565 × 1,644 against 11,138 × 3,829 for planar at full scale), so far less of the file is padding and a crop to a clean rectangle loses much less.

    For quality, the close-ups below take the same 560 × 380 window of output pixels around the same two landmarks. On the plane, those pixels cover a much smaller piece of the scene and are stretched up to several times, so the detail is soft and smeared sideways. On the cylinder the same window shows a wider, sharper view, because each output pixel is close to one photo pixel and was sampled from the original image only once. The Golden Gate pair looks much more alike, which is expected: the tower is about 23° from the middle frame's axis, against 38° for the pine, and tan θ only pulls away from θ quickly at larger angles.

    1. Exposure matching

    Before blending, each side frame gets one gain per color channel so that its median color in the overlap matches the middle frame. I do the matching in roughly linear light (raising values to the 2.2 power first) so the gain behaves like a change in exposure rather than a contrast change. The gains it found:

    2. Two-band blending

    Feathering averages everything, including fine detail, so any misalignment turns into a double image. The two-band version splits each warped frame into a low-pass part (Gaussian, σ = 8 px, built from repeated box filters with cumulative sums) and the high-frequency remainder. Low frequencies are blended with the feather weights so color still transitions smoothly; high frequencies are taken only from whichever frame has the most weight at that pixel. Sharp edges come from one photo, so they cannot double.

    It does not fix the alignment. The pillar's left edge still jumps at the seam, because the two frames really disagree about where it is. It just stops the disagreement from showing up as a ghost.

    07 / B.1 Harris corners and ANMS

    Corners worth keeping

    A corner is a spot that changes if you nudge the window in any direction. A photo has tens of thousands. I need a couple of thousand, and I need them spread out.

    For detection I used the Harris code that comes with the assignment. It scores every pixel with a corner strength (det / trace of the smoothed gradient matrix, the same harmonic-mean measure the MOPS paper uses) and returns every local maximum in a 3×3 neighbourhood, leaving out a 20 px border. On the 1800 px middle frame of plate II that is 62,455 points, and most of them are noise in the sky and other flat areas. Like the paper, I only keep maxima above a strength threshold (0.01, with pixel values between 0 and 1). That leaves 15,060 corners.

    Taking the 2,000 strongest would be the obvious next step, and it is a bad one. Corner strength follows contrast, so the strongest corners pile up on the backlit treetop and the iron window grille and leave the lawn and most of the facade empty. If the part of the photo that overlaps its neighbour happens to be a low-contrast area, there is nothing left there to match.

    Interactive on the live site: switch between every Harris corner, the strongest N and the N chosen by ANMS, and change N. The static figure below shows the same three views.

    adaptive non-maximal suppression

    ri = minj ‖xi − xj‖ over the corners j with  f(xi) < 0.9 f(xj)

    Every corner gets a suppression radius ri: the distance to the nearest corner that is clearly stronger, where clearly means that even 0.9 times its strength f still beats mine. The strongest corner in the photo has an infinite radius. A corner that is the best thing for 80 px around has radius 80. A corner sitting right next to a stronger one has a radius of a few pixels. Keeping the 2,000 largest radii is the same as growing one suppression radius until exactly 2,000 corners survive, except that there is nothing to search for. In this frame that radius comes out at 15 px.

    In code, I sort the corners by strength first. Then the corners allowed to suppress corner i are simply the start of the list, and the distances can be computed in blocks with NumPy. The café frames have between 23,511 and 28,640 corners above the threshold and still take only a few seconds each.

    08 / B.2 Feature descriptors

    A corner's fingerprint

    To find the same corner in another photo I need something to compare. Each corner gets a tiny 8 by 8 picture of its neighbourhood.

    Around every kept corner I take a 40×40 px window from a blurred grayscale copy of the photo and read one sample every 5 px. That gives an 8×8 patch, 64 numbers. The blur (a Gaussian with σ = 2.5 px) is the important part. Reading every fifth pixel of a sharp image would alias, and a soft, low-frequency patch barely changes when the detector puts the corner a pixel or two off in the other photo. The patches are axis-aligned with no rotation handling, which works here because I held the phone level for every frame.

    Each patch is then normalized to zero mean and unit standard deviation. Subtracting the mean removes brightness, dividing by the spread removes contrast, so the same spot still matches when the two photos were exposed differently. In the sunset set frame 01 is about 1.7 stops brighter than frame 02, and its corners match anyway. Corners whose window would stick out of the image are dropped, and so are flat patches (standard deviation under 0.001), where dividing by almost nothing would only amplify noise.

      09 / B.3 Feature matching

      Asking for a second opinion

      The closest descriptor in the other photo is not automatically a match. What counts is how much closer it is than the runner-up.

      For every descriptor in a side frame I compute the squared distance to every descriptor in the middle frame and pick out the nearest and the second nearest. The distance to the nearest one is a poor test by itself. A patch of sky or foliage is close to hundreds of other patches, while a distinctive corner can be a correct match at a fairly large distance. Lowe's idea, which the paper adopts, is to compare the best candidate with the second best. If the nearest neighbour is the right one, the runner-up is some unrelated patch and much further away. If the nearest neighbour is wrong, the two are about equally bad.

      So a match is kept only when the ratio of the two squared distances, 1-NN over 2-NN, is below 0.6. In Figure 6b of the paper wrong matches are almost absent below a ratio of 0.4 and overtake the correct ones at about 0.65, so 0.6 sits just under the crossover. I also require the two features to be each other's nearest neighbour, which removes cases where several corners all pick the same partner.

      To check that threshold on my own photos I rebuilt the figure. Once the homographies were known, I labelled every nearest-neighbour pair in the six photo pairs as correct or incorrect: does the final homography send the corner to within 3 px of its nearest neighbour? Only 1,462 of the 11,740 pairs are correct, because most corners lie outside the overlap and have no true partner at all.

      The two distributions have the same shape as in the paper. With the threshold at 0.6, 79% of the correct pairs survive and 6.3% of the incorrect ones slip through. The ones that slip through are what RANSAC is for.

      10 / B.4 RANSAC

      Letting the matches vote

      After the ratio test a good share of the matches is still wrong, and least squares listens to every one of them. RANSAC only listens to matches that agree with each other.

      4-point RANSAC, 5,000 rounds

      1. Pick four matches at random.
      2. Skip the sample if two of its points coincide or three are nearly on a line, in either photo. Four such points do not pin down a homography.
      3. Fit the exact homography through the four with computeH from Part A. If it sends any of its own four points behind the camera, throw it away too.
      4. Send every matched corner through it and count the inliers: the matches that land within 3 px of their partner.
      5. Keep the largest inlier set seen so far.

      At the end, refit by least squares on all inliers of the winner (computeH again, now overdetermined), recount the inliers, and repeat until the set stops changing.

      How many rounds are enough? If a fraction w of the matches is good, four random ones are all good with probability w4, and the chance of never drawing a clean sample in N rounds is (1 − w4)N. The hardest pair here is in the café, where only 31% of the matches end up as inliers. That needs about 484 rounds for 99% confidence. The building needs 13. I run 5,000 everywhere and fix the random seed, so rerunning the code gives the same mosaics.

      12 / B.5 Bells & whistles: panorama recognition

      Sorting the shoebox

      So far I told the code which photos belong together and in which order. For the extra I took that away too. All 13 photos of the project go in as one unordered pile.

      The pile is the nine frames from above, the two extra sunset frames from Part A's cylinder, and the two rectification photos, which do not belong to any panorama. I extract features for every photo and run the matcher and RANSAC on all 78 pairs. Two photos count as overlapping when RANSAC finds at least 20 inliers between them. The number is easy to pick. Unrelated photos never got more than 5 inliers (four random matches always agree with their own homography, and now and then a fifth lands close), and the weakest real overlap has 26.

      Linked photos form groups, and each group is a panorama: 3 of them, with the book and the menu left over. Inside a group I keep the strongest links as a tree, take the photo in the middle of the tree as the reference, and chain the homographies toward it. The left-to-right order falls out of where each photo lands. Nobody says in advance how wide a discovered panorama will be, so they are rendered on a cylinder with the mapper from Part A. The five-frame skyline is the same set I stitched by hand for Part A's extra, this time with no clicks and no order given.

      13 / reflection

      Behind the frame

      The biggest surprise was how much the result depended on the points rather than the code. Once computeH and the warps passed their tests, every visible problem in a mosaic traced back to a correspondence: a point on the wrong corner, or a point on something at a different depth from the rest. The per-point residuals were the most useful debugging tool I had. They flagged the pillar points in the café before I had looked at a single mosaic.

      Inverse warping makes sense once you think from the output's side. Every output pixel needs a value, so ask where it came from. The masks follow from the same idea. Without them a black pixel and an empty one look identical, and feathering would average real image with blank canvas near every border.

      Nearest neighbor versus bilinear was less dramatic than I expected at normal size, because none of these warps enlarge the photo much. Zoomed in, the stair-steps are obvious, and bilinear costs about two and a half times as much. That is an easy trade for anything meant to be looked at.

      Feathering fixed the seams wherever the alignment was good (plate II is basically seamless) and made things worse in one specific way where it was not, by turning misalignment into ghosts. Exposure matching mattered more than I expected for the sunset. The cylinder extra showed how much of plate I's bow-tie was just the flat projection: with five frames the planar result is mostly empty canvas and smeared edges, and wrapping the same homographies around a cylinder fixed both. The café is the one limit no projection can fix; it really needed the phone to pivot more carefully about the lens.

      After reel B

      Reel B changed how I see reel A. Clicking points felt like the careful, trustworthy way to do it, and for the building it was: my few facade points still beat a few hundred automatic matches that mostly landed on the tree in front. The sunset went the other way. There, hundreds of automatic matches spread over the whole overlap gave a more believable homography than eight careful clicks bunched into one corner, and measuring both against a pure camera rotation is what told me so.

      The steps I expected to be hard were not. Harris was provided, a descriptor is a blur and a slice, and RANSAC is about fifteen lines. What took thought was everything around them: a strength threshold so that ANMS does not spend its budget on noise in the sky, enough interest points that the café still has something to match, and looking at where the inliers are before trusting a homography. RANSAC answers the question of which group of matches agrees the most. In the café that was a concrete pillar, not the wall I cared about.

      The limits are the things I skipped on purpose. There is no rotation or scale invariance, so this works because every frame was taken level, from one spot, at one zoom. And a homography is still only right for a single plane, or for a camera that turns without moving.
