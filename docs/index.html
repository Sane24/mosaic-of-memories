Nine photographs from one afternoon around Berkeley, a few dozen points I matched by hand, and a 3×3 matrix for every pair. This is my write-up for Part A of the image mosaicing project: recovering homographies, warping with my own nearest-neighbor and bilinear samplers, rectifying three views of flat objects, and blending three panoramas.

Begin with the frames

01 / A.1 Shoot the pictures

Fragments

Three sets of three overlapping frames, laid out like a contact sheet. The dashed outline on each print is the part of the scene its neighbor also saw, computed from the recovered homography.

Everything was shot on my iPhone 14 Pro in a single afternoon. For each mosaic I took a middle frame and then turned left and right from the same spot, so each set is three frames taken within a few seconds of each other (the sunset set spans about a minute and a half). The campus and café sets use the main 24 mm-equivalent lens. For the sunset I used the 2× setting (46 to 48 mm equivalent), which makes the bay bigger in frame but leaves less overlap between shots.

A homography lines two photos up exactly only when the camera rotates about its own optical center or the scene is flat. A phone never pivots perfectly about its lens, so how well each set works depends mostly on how far away the subject is. The phone also picked its own exposure for every frame, which matters later: in the sunset set the shutter speed ranges from 1/10870 s to 1/4184 s.

02 / A.2 Recover homographies

Finding the same points

Two photos of the same place disagree about where everything is. A handful of landmarks they agree on is enough to work out the rest.

For each pair I looked for landmarks I could place exactly in both photos: corners of window frames, letters carved into the facade, the top of a Golden Gate tower, the corners of the menu screens. I tried to spread them across the whole overlap instead of clustering them on one object, since points bunched together only constrain the transformation near that spot. Each side frame is matched to the middle frame, so every mosaic has two point sets and two homographies.

I placed the points on the full-resolution 4032 × 3024 photos and saved them to JSON in original-pixel coordinates. The pipeline works on a downsized copy (long side 1800 px), so it rescales the points on load and never needs them re-picked. All the pixel errors below are at that 1800 px working size.

    Hover or focus a landmark to follow its thread. Numbers in brackets are the leftover error for that point, in pixels.

    Interactive on the live site. The printed version shows the saved correspondence figures below.

    the model

    A homography maps a point (x, y) in one photo to (u, v) in the other through homogeneous coordinates:

    wuwvw = h11h12h13h21h22h23h31h321 xy1

    Setting h33 = 1 fixes the scale, leaving eight unknowns. Dividing out w and multiplying through gives two equations that are linear in the unknowns:

    h11x + h12y + h13 − h31xu − h32yu = u h21x + h22y + h23 − h31xv − h32yv = v

    stacking every correspondence

    Ah = b

    x1y11000−x1u1−y1u1 000x1y11−x1v1−y1v1 ⋮⋮⋮⋮⋮⋮⋮⋮ xnyn1000−xnun−ynun 000xnyn1−xnvn−ynvn h11h12h13h21h22h23h31h32 = u1v1⋮unvn

    Every pair of points adds two rows, so n correspondences give a 2n × 8 system. Four points are the minimum, and with exactly four the system is square and the homography passes through them exactly. With 7 to 14 points per pair the system is overdetermined, and computeH solves it by least squares (np.linalg.lstsq, minimizing ‖Ah − b‖²). That is the point of using extra matches: a pixel or two of error in where I put a point gets averaged against the others instead of going straight into H.

    One practical detail: before building A, I shift and scale each point set so its centroid is at the origin and its average distance from it is √2, then undo that transform on the result. Raw pixel coordinates make the xu columns about a million times larger than the constant column, and the normalization keeps the solve well conditioned.

    the pillar problem

    For the right café pair I started with ten points. Three of them, on the concrete pillar and a hanging lamp, disagreed with the rest by 20 to 40 pixels whichever subset I fit. Those objects are much closer to the camera than the back wall, so they shift differently when the phone moves, and no single homography can satisfy both depths. I kept the seven points on the back wall and the menu screens. The wall lines up, and the pillar ghosts a little instead of everything being slightly off.

    03 / A.3 Warp the images

    Shifting perspective

    A photo rarely sees a flat thing straight on. If I know four corners of something that is really a rectangle, one homography turns it back to face me.

    I rectified three things: a book cover and two printed menus standing on a counter. For each I picked the four corners (top-left, top-right, bottom-right, bottom-left of the real object) and mapped them to the corners of an upright rectangle. With exactly four points, computeH gets a square 8 × 8 system and the corners land exactly where I asked.

    I did not want to guess the rectangle's proportions, so I estimated them from the photo. The EXIF data says 24 mm equivalent, about 1248 px at the working size. Shooting a ray from the camera through each corner and requiring the four 3D points to form a parallelogram fixes the true side lengths up to scale. That gives width/height 0.659 for the book and 0.775 for the front menu. The menu looks like a US-letter sheet, which would be 8.5/11 = 0.773, and the recovered corner angles are 89.3° and 90.1°, so I trust the estimates.

    the hidden corner

    This menu's left edge is behind the front stand, so two of its corners are not visible. I fit straight lines to the top, right and bottom edges of the sheet. The right edge and a printed vertical line on the sheet meet far below the photo at the vertical vanishing point, and the hidden left edge has to pass through that point too. Assuming the sheet is the same size as the front menu (same stand, same paper), that fixes where the left edge is. The bottom-left corner it predicts lands within about 25 px of where the sheet visibly meets the stand, which is a good check. The strip on the left of the result is the front stand, which really is in front of the sheet.

    Why inverse warping

    Both warps work backwards. For every pixel of the output I apply H−1 to find where it came from in the source photo and sample there. Pushing source pixels forward through H instead would leave holes wherever the warp stretches the image, and pile several pixels onto one where it shrinks. Going backwards, every output pixel gets exactly one value. Output pixels whose source location falls outside the photo are marked invalid in a mask rather than painted black.

    The source location is almost never a whole pixel. warpImageNearestNeighbor rounds it to the closest pixel center. warpImageBilinear takes the four pixels around it and weights them by (1−dx)(1−dy), dx(1−dy), (1−dx)dy and dx dy, where dx and dy are the fractional offsets. Both are written with NumPy indexing and arithmetic only.

    Quality and speed

    At normal viewing size the two results look almost identical, because these rectifications only enlarge the photo slightly. The difference shows up on edges once you zoom in: nearest neighbor gives jagged, blocky diagonals and bilinear gives smooth ones.

    Bilinear is slower because it reads four pixels and does several multiplies per output pixel instead of one lookup. Median of three runs on my machine: 34 ms vs 79 ms for the book and 43 ms vs 101 ms for the menu, so bilinear costs about 2.4× to 2.3× more. Both are fast enough not to matter here, so I used bilinear for everything else, including the mosaics.

    04 / A.4 Blend into mosaics

    Stitching the world back together

    Each mosaic lives in its middle frame's coordinates. The side frames are warped into that frame by chaining their homographies, and everything is blended on one shared canvas.

    To size the canvas, I push the four corners of every frame through its homography, take the bounding box, and offset the output so its top-left pixel sits at that box's minimum (usually negative) corner. Each frame is then inverse-warped onto the full canvas with bilinear sampling and its own validity mask. Before blending I scale each side frame so its median color in the overlap matches the middle frame, then feather all three together (details in section 05). The bow-tie shape is real: a flat projection stretches anything far from the middle frame's viewing direction, so the outer corners fan out.

    05 / A.4 Blending, plus bells & whistles

    The invisible seams

    A mask decides what each frame is allowed to show. Blending decides how much.

    Once all three frames are on the same canvas, something has to decide what happens where they overlap. Pasting one on top of the other (the hard-seam versions above) leaves a visible edge wherever brightness or alignment differs even slightly.

    Instead, each frame gets a weight that is 1 at its center and falls off linearly to 0 at its border. I compute it as the distance to the nearest edge of the original photo, measured in that photo's own pixels, so it warps along with the frame. The output pixel is then the weighted average of every frame that covers it:

    I(p) = Σi mi(p) wi(p) Ii(p)Σi mi(p) wi(p)

    mi is the validity mask from the inverse warp: 1 if the pixel really came from inside photo i, 0 if it fell off the edge. That is what keeps empty canvas from being averaged in, and it is why a genuinely dark pixel (the black menu screens, the shadowed rooftops) still counts as data.

    each frame's share of plate II, after normalizing the weights

    bells & whistles

    Two extras that actually helped

    1. Exposure matching

    Before blending, each side frame gets one gain per color channel so that its median color in the overlap matches the middle frame. I do the matching in roughly linear light (raising values to the 2.2 power first) so the gain behaves like a change in exposure rather than a contrast change. The gains it found:

    plateleft frameright frame
    I0.26 / 0.29 / 0.310.91 / 0.95 / 1.00
    II1.43 / 1.43 / 1.470.89 / 0.97 / 1.11
    III1.31 / 1.46 / 1.800.80 / 0.80 / 0.74

    R / G / B. The reference frame always keeps a gain of 1. This is global, so vignetting inside a single frame is still there.

    2. Two-band blending

    Feathering averages everything, including fine detail, so any misalignment turns into a double image. The two-band version splits each warped frame into a low-pass part (Gaussian, σ = 8 px, built from repeated box filters with cumulative sums) and the high-frequency remainder. Low frequencies are blended with the feather weights so color still transitions smoothly; high frequencies are taken only from whichever frame has the most weight at that pixel. Sharp edges come from one photo, so they cannot double.

    It does not fix the alignment. The pillar's left edge still jumps at the seam, because the two frames really disagree about where it is. It just stops the disagreement from showing up as a ghost.

    06 / reflection

    Behind the frame

    The biggest surprise was how much the result depended on the points rather than the code. Once computeH and the warps passed their tests, every visible problem in a mosaic traced back to a correspondence: a point on the wrong corner, or a point on something at a different depth from the rest. The per-point residuals were the most useful debugging tool I had. They flagged the pillar points in the café before I had looked at a single mosaic.

    Inverse warping makes sense once you think from the output's side. Every output pixel needs a value, so ask where it came from. The masks follow from the same idea. Without them a black pixel and an empty one look identical, and feathering would average real image with blank canvas near every border.

    Nearest neighbor versus bilinear was less dramatic than I expected at normal size, because none of these warps enlarge the photo much. Zoomed in, the stair-steps are obvious, and bilinear costs about two and a half times as much. That is an easy trade for anything meant to be looked at.

    Feathering fixed the seams wherever the alignment was good (plate II is basically seamless) and made things worse in one specific way where it was not, by turning misalignment into ghosts. Exposure matching mattered more than I expected for the sunset. The limits are still visible: the stretched ends of plates I and II would look better with a cylindrical projection, which I did not implement, and the café really needed the phone to pivot more carefully about the lens.
